\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Human intervention supplies useful authority only when the subsequent effect
respects the reply's scope. The completed studies expose distinct obstacles:
agents can fail to consult, act unsafely after consulting, or omit work despite
valid approval. A single attack-success or help-seeking score cannot distinguish
these outcomes. \bench{} makes them separately observable, and SAIL-HIL turns
their separation into a controller design: interpret the reply, bind a permit,
and retain the original task obligations.

Benchmark validity and controller validity require different evidence. A frozen
reply contract makes comparisons reproducible, but the nearby-scope audit shows
why construction labels still need independent semantic review. Conversely,
validating generated replies does not validate the controller's interpretation
of them. The earlier runtime studies support lower unsafe execution on valid
pairs, while task omissions and uneven failures restrict broader claims. The
stateful clarification and recovery comparisons must establish which mechanisms
improve safe task progress and at what interaction cost.

The setting remains controlled: replies are generated, consequential effects
are simulated, new tasks reuse known implementation families, and one actor route anchors
the planned stateful matrix. Human annotation is needed to resolve semantic
validity; unseen-family tasks and additional native agents are needed to test
generalization. The present controller studies compare internal ablations and
Prompt controls rather than adapted external runtime guards. Matched clean
workflows are also needed to estimate false interventions. Uninstrumented-tool
analysis is needed to assess mediation coverage. Within these boundaries, the benchmark and controller provide a
concrete way to study human feedback as an auditable change in task authority,
with safety and authorized completion evaluated together.
