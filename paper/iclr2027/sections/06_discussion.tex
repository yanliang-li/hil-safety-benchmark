\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The prompt comparison exposes both missing consultation and misuse of replies. The initial SAIL v3 controller reduces valid-run ASR further, but lowers benign completion and strict post-feedback success while adding questions and failures. Its paired joint-utility interval includes zero. These results support a safety--utility tradeoff, not a uniform improvement in human control.

With human replies, SAIL v3 safely executes 58 of 141 matched approval cases, versus zero without replies. Yet its 41 remaining unsafe runs all follow matched consultation: 22 transfer approval across scope, and 19 accept approval for prohibited effects. Better consultation coverage therefore does not ensure correct authority use.

The evidence is limited to 20 reused tasks with fixed synthetic replies and simulated consequences. Framework prompts, context settings, and serving time differ; the third-party model routes are not verified weights. Failures are unevenly distributed, especially for GLM, and conditional post-feedback groups change with the defense. Semantic recognition, question accuracy, consultation necessity, and deliverable quality need independent review. The four-reply core does not test objectively false advice, genuine partial authorization, or continuations from an identical pre-response state.

\bench{} connects intervention decisions to observable tool effects. Its results support evaluating three stages together: requesting the right help, interpreting its authority, and carrying it through to execution. SAIL follows these stages and preserves some authorized execution that its no-human version cannot. The revised controller preserves original authority and proposes task recovery. Its third-round performance remains pending; implementation tests do not establish an empirical improvement.
